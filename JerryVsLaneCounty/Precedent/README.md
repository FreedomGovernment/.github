# Case Law for Jerry Nelson v. Lane County Government

Research index for proposed independent federal claims concerning alleged demolition, municipal nuisance penalties, notice, and disability-related modification. The linked PDFs are downloaded copies, not case filings or evidence that any proposition in Jerry's account is established.

## Most directly relevant

- *Thomas v. County of Humboldt*, 124 F.4th 1179 (9th Cir. 2024): in a county code-abatement case, the published opinion allowed an Excessive Fines Clause challenge to large accruing administrative penalties and demolition orders to proceed on sufficiently pleaded punitive character, injury, and excessiveness. It was a pleading-stage decision, not a determination that Jerry's penalties are excessive. Determine the County's actual penalty schedule, whether sanctions accrued, what was paid, and what portion was reimbursement rather than punishment.[5]
- *McGary v. City of Portland*, 386 F.3d 1259, 1265–70 (9th Cir. 2004): a person with a documented disability adequately pleaded Title II ADA and Fair Housing Act reasonable-accommodation claims where a city denied a request for more time to clear a nuisance; code enforcement can be covered even when compliance is mandatory. It does not excuse unsafe conditions or establish Jerry's disability, County knowledge, request, reasonable alternative, or causal injury. The PDF copy is hosted by the Center for HIV Law and Policy, not the Ninth Circuit.[6]
- *Lavan v. City of Los Angeles*, 693 F.3d 1022 (9th Cir. 2012): seizure and summary destruction of unabandoned personal possessions may implicate Fourth Amendment and Fourteenth Amendment protections despite a code violation. This involved personal belongings, not an adjudicated house demolition; investigate who destroyed what, whether it was abandoned, and the process and emergency grounds asserted.[7]

## Notice, fines, and attribution

- *Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co.*, 339 U.S. 306, 314–15 (1950): notice must be reasonably calculated, in the circumstances, to apprise interested persons and afford an opportunity to object. Need the actual pre-abatement and pre-demolition notices and the procedures afforded, not just a later judgment's mistaken mailing address.[1]
- *Jones v. Flowers*, 547 U.S. 220 (2006): if the government learns that certified-mail notice of a property sale was returned unclaimed, practicable additional reasonable steps may be required. It does not establish that Lane County's earlier notices failed or that certified mail was returned; the photographed September 2026 service certificates assert mailing to the corrected address, not actual receipt.[2]
- *Timbs v. Indiana*, 586 U.S. 146 (2019): the Excessive Fines Clause applies to states via the Fourteenth Amendment. The Court did not decide that the particular forfeiture before it was excessive, much less whether a nuisance assessment is punitive.[3]
- *United States v. Bajakajian*, 524 U.S. 321, 334 (1998): a punitive forfeiture is unconstitutional when grossly disproportionate to the gravity of the offense. Identify the government interest, underlying violation, harm, sanction authority, and the penalty-versus-remediation split before using the analogy.[4]
- *Monell v. Department of Social Services*, 436 U.S. 658, 690–94 (1978): a county may face § 1983 liability for a violation caused by official policy, custom, or a decision of an authorized policymaker, not merely because it employed the person involved. Identify the entity that ordered and executed the demolition: the Captain's account refers to a “City,” while the named federal defendant is the County.[8]
- *Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Saudi Basic Industries Corp.*, 544 U.S. 280, 284, 291–93 (2005): federal district court is not an appellate venue to reject an earlier state judgment; independent federal claims require their own analysis, and preclusion is a separate issue. The federal action's filing date, state judgment's actual entry, and relief sought matter; a refund of sums paid solely under a state judgment could raise a different issue from damages for earlier County conduct.[9]

## Needed for Jerry's review

1. Obtain the actual ECF 17 screening/deficiency order and current federal docket, including any later scheduling order. The case-folder transcription of ECF 20 says the first amended complaint is due within 14 days of September 16, 2026; under Rule 6(a), that ordinarily yields Wednesday, September 30, 2026, if no subsequent order changes it. Do not treat a photograph transcription as verification of the live docket.
2. Separate County from City (identify exact city, if any). Obtain abatement notices, dates, delivery evidence, hearing notices and decisions, demolition authorization, contracts, invoices, engineer report, and photographs or other contemporaneous records. A mistake in the later money award does not by itself prove defective initial service.
3. Obtain an itemized assessment distinguishing daily civil penalties, demolition or cleanup costs, interest, attorney fees, collections, unpaid balance, and amounts actually paid. The photographed proposed corrected judgment states $139,172.12, but that amount is not an established $100,000 paid fine.
4. Ask Jerry what he communicated about his disability, to whom and when; the accommodation or extension he actually requested; the County or City response; any immediate public-safety hazard; and his actual losses from demolition. A television appearance is not a clinical or legal finding.
5. Confirm state case 25CV55934's actual judgment, entry and appeal posture and whether sheriff's sale occurred. Confirm whether an additional municipal defendant can properly be added, and whether relief seeks damages for independent actions or in substance reversal of that judgment.
